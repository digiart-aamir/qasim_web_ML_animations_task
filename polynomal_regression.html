Metric formulas & explanations
R-squared (R²)
1 − SSE / SST measures the share of variation explained by the model; it can be negative.
Adjusted R-squared
1 − (1 − R²)(n − 1)/(n − p − 1) adjusts R² for sample size and the p polynomial predictors.
Mean Absolute Error (MAE)
Σ|yᵢ − ŷᵢ| / n is the average absolute error, in Y units.
Mean Squared Error (MSE)
Σ(yᵢ − ŷᵢ)² / n is the average squared error, in squared Y units.
Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE)
√MSE is the root-mean-square error, in Y units.
Sum of Squared Errors (SSE)
Σ(yᵢ − ŷᵢ)² is the total squared residual error.
Total Sum of Squares (SST)
Σ(yᵢ − ȳ)² is the total variation in observed Y around its mean.

Here, n is the observation count, p is the polynomial degree (predictors excluding the intercept), and each residual is yᵢ − ŷᵢ. R² is undefined when SST = 0; adjusted R² is undefined when SST = 0 or n ≤ p + 1. A degree-p model needs at least p + 1 observations to fit. With Ridge λ > 0, the conventional adjusted R² does not account for shrinkage.

Learning Guide

Polynomial Regression, Explained

This guide introduces the ideas behind the model in this analyzer. Polynomial regression can describe curved trends, but its results should always be checked against the data and interpreted within a sensible range.

What is polynomial regression?

It is a regression method that predicts a numeric response Y from an input X using powers of X. Those extra powers let the fitted relationship bend instead of forcing it to be a straight line.

ŷ = b₀ + b₁x + b₂x² + … + bₙxⁿ

The fitted value is written as ŷ; the difference between an observed value and its fit is a residual.

Why use it?

Use a polynomial when a scatterplot or domain knowledge suggests a smooth curved relationship that a straight line cannot represent well. It can capture bends and turning points while keeping X numeric.

Start with a low degree and add complexity only when the data support it. Polynomial curves can behave poorly beyond the observed X range.

How does it work?

  • Choose a degree n and create model terms 1, x, x², …, xⁿ.
  • Estimate one coefficient for each term from the observed pairs.
  • Use the fitted equation to calculate predicted values and residuals.

This analyzer centers and scales X for numerical stability while fitting; it also displays original-X coefficients when representable.

Linear vs. polynomial regression

A degree-1 polynomial is ordinary straight-line regression: ŷ = b₀ + b₁x. Degree 2 adds an x² term; higher degrees add more powers and allow more curve shapes.

Both methods are linear in their unknown coefficients and can be fitted by least squares. Polynomial regression is not automatically better—it is simply more flexible.

What does degree mean?

The degree is the largest power of X in the equation. A degree-1 model has 2 coefficients, degree 2 has 3, and degree n has n + 1.

Higher degree can add bends, but also makes the fit more sensitive to noise. A model needs enough distinct X values to estimate its terms reliably.

What do the coefficients mean?

b₀ is the intercept; each later coefficient weights a corresponding power of X. The intercept is the predicted Y at X = 0 when that point is meaningful for the dataset.

Interpret the terms together: powers of X are related, so a coefficient alone is not a causal effect. Standardized coefficients describe the centered/scaled model; original-X coefficients express the same curve in raw units.

Least-squares estimation

For each observation, the residual is eᵢ = yᵢ − ŷᵢ. Least squares chooses coefficients that make the total squared residual as small as possible:

SSE = ∑ (yᵢ − ŷᵢ)²

Squaring makes larger misses count more. This analyzer uses QR decomposition rather than directly inverting the design matrix.

How should a model be evaluated?

R² summarizes fit relative to the variation in Y; MAE and RMSE describe typical error in Y units. Residual plots can reveal visible patterns that the summary numbers miss.

Training metrics describe the data used to fit the model, not guaranteed future accuracy. When possible, also evaluate on observations held aside from fitting.

Underfitting and overfitting

Underfitting: the degree is too simple to capture the relationship, often leaving a systematic pattern in the residual plot.

Overfitting: a very flexible curve follows random noise in the training data and may predict poorly on new data, especially outside the observed range.

Compare reasonable degrees and use validation data; a high training R² alone is not proof of a useful model.

Practical applications

Polynomial models can be useful for calibration curves, engineering response measurements, smooth growth trends, dose-response studies, and approximating physical relationships over a measured interval.

Use domain knowledge, inspect residuals, and be cautious with extrapolation. A polynomial is not a good choice for every nonlinear or discontinuous relationship.
